\begin{abstract}
Attention forgets everything beyond its window, but the text a language model reads stays on the same topic, characters and vocabulary for far longer.
We ask whether a small language model that keeps learning online, one SGD step on each chunk after predicting it, uses part of its weights as a cache of its current context.
Pythia-14M reads PG-19 books in independent chunks of 256 tokens.
Online learning lowers its loss by $0.099 \pm 0.006$ nats/token, and by $0.301$ for rare tokens last seen in an earlier chunk under 1k tokens back.
This long-range gain is local (only 18\% of it survives shuffling the chunks), tied mostly to the current book rather than to the recency of single tokens, more than a count cache beyond 1k tokens, and stored in the MLPs below the output layer.
Making the cache more aggressive (higher learning rates, a single layer, decay) gains more on recent material but loses much more on everything else, at every level of locality we tested.
A small decaying fast part on one middle MLP, added on top of the slow learner, gives a small but robust extra gain with strong locality ($+0.012 \pm 0.001$ on books read in order, $+0.021 \pm 0.003$ on long books) and costs little without it ($-0.008$); meta-learning per-tensor rates and decays learns almost nothing beyond its starting values.
Over 200k-token books, online learning closes about half of the gap between Pythia-14M and a frozen Pythia-31M, but Pythia-31M learning online gains just as much: at these sizes, online learning does not favor the smaller model.
These are preliminary results with one seed, plain SGD and few books.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

What a model reads at inference time has strong temporal locality.
A book keeps its characters, places and turns of phrase for hundreds of thousands of tokens; a conversation, a code base or a document collection behaves the same way.
Computer memory hierarchies have long exploited this property with a small cache that holds the recently used data \cite{denning1968working}.
A transformer language model has such a cache in its attention window, but the window is short: anything older is forgotten.

Weights updated online could keep a memory of the current context beyond the window.
Dynamic evaluation \cite{krause2018dynamic,krause2019dynamic,rannentriki2024revisiting} and recent test-time training methods \cite{feng2026inplace,bansal2026lets,tandon2025endtoend} do this and report lower perplexity or better long-context performance.
What is less clear is what kind of memory the updated weights hold.
If online learning turns part of a model into a cache, the gain should be largest for material seen recently and fall with distance (recency), new material should push old material out (eviction, the useful side of the catastrophic interference that sequential training causes \cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}), and the general knowledge in the weights should survive.
Alternatively, the gain may come mostly from adapting to the domain or style of the text in general, which needs no memory of the recent past.
A cache would also matter most where capacity is scarce: a small model could rely on it more than a larger one.

We study these questions with a deliberately simple setup and small models, where controls are cheap.
Pythia models \cite{biderman2023pythia} read PG-19 test books \cite{rae2020compressive} in chunks of 256 tokens, each scored with no context from the previous chunk, so anything that helps beyond 256 tokens has to come through the weights; after scoring a chunk, the model takes one SGD step on it.
Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Controls that split the online gain}: by distance to the token's previous occurrence, into a local part (which needs the order of the text) and a global part (a shuffled stream), into same-book and earlier-book memory, and against a count cache. The long-range gain is local and mostly tied to the current book.
\item The \textbf{location of the memory}: updating only the MLPs, or one middle layer's MLP, keeps most of the long-range gain; the output layer alone does not.
\item \textbf{Locality as a variable}: streams that interleave $K$ books, which show that making the cache more aggressive does not pay off at any locality level tested, while a small decaying fast part on top of the slow learner does, by a small margin.
\item \textbf{Long contexts and model size}: on 200k-token books, online learning is worth about half the step from Pythia-14M to Pythia-31M, but Pythia-31M gains as much from it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Dynamic evaluation.}
Krause et al.\ \cite{krause2018dynamic} adapt a recurrent language model to the recent history of the sequence it is predicting with gradient steps at test time; applied to Transformer-XL the gains are smaller than for weaker models, which the authors take to suggest that Transformers already model re-occurring patterns better than earlier architectures \cite{krause2019dynamic}.
Rannen-Triki et al.\ \cite{rannentriki2024revisiting} revisit dynamic evaluation for larger models and frame it as memory in weights, a form of context-length extension, with the largest gains under distribution shift.
We use the same prequential protocol (score, then update) and ask what the updated weights remember and where.

\paragraph{Cache language models.}
Kuhn and De Mori \cite{kuhn1990cache} mix a cache of recently seen words into an $n$-gram model; Grave et al.\ \cite{grave2017improving} store recent hidden states and their next words and mix the resulting distribution into a neural model.
Our unigram cache is the simplest member of this family and the baseline a ``cache in the weights'' should beat.

\paragraph{Test-time training for language models.}
Test-time training adapts a model to each test input with a self-supervised loss \cite{sun2020test}.
For language models, In-Place TTT \cite{feng2026inplace} treats the final projection of the MLP blocks as fast weights updated with a next-token objective; qTTT \cite{bansal2026lets} updates only the query projections on the long context; TTT-E2E \cite{tandon2025endtoend} meta-learns a model for test-time training on long context.
Our finding that MLPs below the output hold the memory is consistent with In-Place TTT's choice of adaptable weights and with the view of feed-forward layers as key-value memories \cite{geva2021transformer}.

\paragraph{Fast weights and learned update rules.}
Fast weights change at inference time to hold short-term memory on top of slow weights \cite{hinton1987using,schmidhuber1992learning,ba2016using}; linear transformers can be read as fast weight programmers \cite{schlag2021linear}.
Meta-SGD \cite{li2017metasgd} learns a learning rate per parameter.
Our slow-plus-fast rule and its meta-learned variant are minimal instances of these ideas applied on top of a pretrained model.

\paragraph{What this work adds.}
Prior work reports the overall gain of online adaptation.
We split it by distance, into local and global parts, by book, against a count cache and by the part of the network that is updated, and we vary the locality of the input and the context length.

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Models.}
Pythia-14M \cite{biderman2023pythia} (14.07M parameters: 6 layers of width 128 plus untied input and output embeddings of 6.44M each), Pythia-31M (30.49M) and Pythia-70M (70.43M), all with 6 layers and the same tokenizer; float32 on CPU.

\paragraph{Data.}
PG-19 test books \cite{rae2020compressive}, in the order the Hugging Face dataset gives them; books under 20k tokens (three short stories) are skipped.
The main stream is the first 25 books, each cut to its first 20k tokens, concatenated (500k tokens).
Two \emph{long} streams use the first test books with at least 100k tokens (5 books cut to 100k) and at least 200k tokens (4 books cut to 200k).
Pythia was trained on the Pile \cite{gao2020pile}, whose PG-19 component has 28{,}602 documents \cite{biderman2022datasheet}, exactly the size of PG-19's training split, so the test books are very likely not in Pythia's training data.
The first learning-rate picks use 2 further test books (the \emph{check books}); all later tuning and meta-training use only PG-19 validation books: 10 for picking (\emph{val-pick}), 26 for meta-training and 6 held out.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
A stream is cut into non-overlapping chunks of $L = 256$ tokens, each scored on its own with no attention to earlier chunks; every token but a chunk's first is predicted from the tokens before it.
Online, after scoring a chunk the model takes one plain SGD step (no momentum or weight decay) on the chunk's mean loss: every token is predicted before it is trained on.
The frozen model reads the same stream without updates.
Some runs update only part of the network (the output embedding, everything else, the MLPs or the attention of all layers, or one layer's MLP).
In the \emph{slow and fast} rule the model predicts with $S + F$, where $F$ starts at 0; after each chunk, with $g$ the gradient at $S + F$, $S \leftarrow S - 10^{-4} g$ on all parameters and $F \leftarrow \lambda F - \alpha g$ on the tensors that have a fast part.
Without a fast part this reproduces plain SGD at $10^{-4}$ exactly.

\paragraph{Locality streams.}
To vary locality, each of the 25 books is chunked on its own (78 chunks; the remainder is dropped) and the same chunks are read in different orders: the books one after the other ($K=1$), groups of 5 books with chunks interleaved round-robin inside a group ($K=5$), all 25 books round-robin ($K=25$), and one random order (shuffled, seed 0).

\paragraph{Learning rates.}
Each learning rate is the one with the lowest mean loss on the check books or on val-pick, from a grid of three or four values; from test 6 on, a pick at the edge of its grid extends the grid once by one step.
For Pythia-14M with all parameters the pick is $10^{-4}$ (check-book loss 4.4775, against 4.5474 frozen).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
The \emph{gain} of a run is the frozen loss minus the run's loss over the same tokens (nats/token); each model is compared with its own frozen loss unless stated.
Each scored token is binned by where the previous occurrence of the same token id is: in the \emph{same chunk} (attention can see it), in an \emph{earlier chunk} under 1k, 1k--10k, 10k--100k or at least 100k tokens back, or \emph{never seen}; the 1000 most frequent ids are left out of the bins (139{,}281 of 498{,}046 scored tokens remain).
Earlier-chunk bins are split by whether that occurrence is in the token's own book or an earlier one.
The \emph{locality} is the gain in reading order minus the gain on the same chunks shuffled: the part of the gain that needs the recent material.
\textbf{Error bars:} all means in the text and tables are token-level; standard errors are computed over books, from the per-book means or, for comparisons of two runs on the same chunks, from the paired per-book differences (books with fewer than 50 tokens in a bin are left out of the SE).
A $\dagger$ marks a difference under 2 SE, which we treat as within noise.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The online gain reaches beyond the window and is local}

Online learning lowers Pythia-14M's loss from 4.3922 to 4.2929 nats/token (Table~\ref{tab:distance}).
For rare tokens last seen in an earlier chunk the gain falls with distance, from $+0.301$ under 1k tokens back to $+0.143$ at 1k--10k and is within noise at 10k--100k; tokens never seen before lose ($-0.062$), as do tokens repeated inside their own chunk ($-0.107$), which attention already copies (Figure~\ref{fig:distance}a).
After each switch to a new book the gain starts near zero and keeps growing over the 20k tokens of a book (Figure~\ref{fig:distance}b).
Shuffling the chunks keeps the training data and removes the order.
The shuffled learner keeps 61\% of the overall gain, which is therefore mostly adaptation to old books in general, but only 18\% of the gain under 1k tokens and 27\% at 1k--10k: the long-range gain is local (locality $+0.247$ and $+0.105$).

\subsection{It is mostly about the current book}

Table~\ref{tab:samebook} splits the earlier-chunk bins by book.
At 1k--10k tokens back, the locality is $+0.117$ for tokens last seen in the same book and $+0.025$ for tokens last seen in an earlier book; same-book tokens 10k--20k back still have $+0.038$, more than closer earlier-book tokens, while earlier-book tokens at 10k--100k have none.
The fall of the gain with distance is therefore largely a fall in the share of same-book tokens, although distance still matters within a book.
The unigram cache depends more on distance than on the book: it gains on same-book tokens at 1k--10k but already loses at 10k--20k, where online learning still gains $+0.065$.

\subsection{More than a count cache}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares online learning with a unigram cache mixed into the frozen model, $p = (1-\lambda) p_\text{model} + \lambda p_\text{cache}$, with exponentially decaying counts updated after each chunk ($\lambda = 0.05$, half-life 1k tokens).
The cache is the better short-range copier ($+0.462$ under 1k tokens, $+0.089$ within the chunk), but from 1k tokens on online learning is better ($+0.143$ against $+0.058$ at 1k--10k), and overall it gains six times more.
The two combine within 10k tokens ($+0.673$ under 1k), but the combination loses beyond, so overall it is no better than online learning alone ($+0.002 \pm 0.002$, within noise).
Updating everything but the output embedding reproduces the full learner; updating only the output embedding is a short-range cache at best ($+0.251$ under 1k at lr $3\times10^{-3}$, only $+0.066$ at 1k--10k).

\subsection{Eviction: a book's memory decays slowly}

Three books A are split into halves A1 and A2 of 10k tokens; online learning reads A1, then $I \in \{0, 10\text{k}, 100\text{k}\}$ tokens of other books, then A2, and a second run replaces A1 with 10k tokens of a different book B1.
The \emph{memory of A} is the loss on A2 after B1 minus the loss on A2 after A1: $+0.038$, $+0.027$ and $+0.019$ nats/token at $I = 0$, 10k and 100k (mean of 3 books, all positive at every $I$; Figure~\ref{fig:eviction}).
The comparison is matched, so the memory of A halves over 100k tokens.
How the two runs get there is open: the A1 run's gain on A2 is flat ($+0.086$, $+0.082$, $+0.085$) while the B1 run's grows ($+0.048$, $+0.055$, $+0.067$), which is consistent both with the A1 run keeping A and with it losing A-specific memory while gaining general adaptation at the same rate.
Updating everything but the output embedding gives a similar memory; the unigram cache keeps $+0.008$ at $I = 0$ and nothing after 10k tokens.

\subsection{The memory lives in the MLPs}

Updating only the MLPs of the six layers (0.79M parameters) keeps most of the long-range gain ($+0.216$ under 1k tokens, $+0.123$ at 1k--10k); updating only attention (0.40M) keeps less ($+0.142$, $+0.092$).
Table~\ref{tab:layers} goes further, updating one layer's MLP (131{,}712 parameters) at a learning rate picked per layer.
Every layer's MLP but the last holds a large part of the memory: the MLP of layer 2 or 4 alone gains $+0.395$ and $+0.357$ under 1k tokens, more than the full learner, while the last layer is clearly the weakest (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}a).
These single-layer runs need higher learning rates, several at the edge of the grid, and pay with lower overall gains and larger losses on tokens not seen recently (layer 2: $+0.055$ overall, $-0.129$ on never-seen tokens); on recent same-book tokens the layer-4 MLP is level with the full learner (difference $+0.008 \pm 0.006$, within noise).

\subsection{Locality as a variable}

The $K$ streams hold the same chunks in orders of decreasing locality.
With plain SGD on all parameters the gain falls steadily as locality decreases: $+0.176$ on the long books, $+0.100$ with the books in order ($K=1$), $+0.074$ and $+0.065$ with 5 and 25 books interleaved, and $+0.062$ shuffled (Table~\ref{tab:aggressive}, Figure~\ref{fig:locality}).
Most of the gain on interleaved streams is the global part that shuffling keeps.

\subsection{Making the cache more aggressive does not pay off}

A more aggressive cache should gain more when locality is strong and lose more when it is weak, so that settings cross over as locality decreases.
They do not (Table~\ref{tab:aggressive}): updating only layer 4's MLP at a higher learning rate, or with a fast part that decays back to the pretrained weights, is worse than plain SGD on every stream, and the order of the settings never changes.
The aggressive settings do gain more on recent same-book tokens ($+0.267$ and $+0.295$ against $+0.210$ on $K=1$), but they lose far more on everything else ($-0.292$ and $-0.234$ against $-0.059$ on never-seen tokens).
The gap to plain SGD narrows as locality grows (for MLP 4 at lr $10^{-2}$: 0.113 shuffled, 0.082 with $K=1$, 0.045 on the long books), so a crossover may exist at stronger locality than we tested.
Decay makes weak locality more costly, not less: it throws away the general adaptation that carries most of the gain there.

\subsection{Slow and fast weights}

A separate fast part does help, if it sits on top of the slow learner.
With the slow part as plain SGD on all parameters and a small fast part ($\alpha = 10^{-3}$, $\lambda = 0.99$, half-life about 18k tokens) on one middle MLP, picked on validation books, the gain over plain SGD is small but robust: $+0.012 \pm 0.001$ with $K=1$, $+0.021 \pm 0.003$ on the long books and $-0.008 \pm 0.000$ shuffled (Table~\ref{tab:dual}).
The extra gain is on recent same-book tokens ($+0.058 \pm 0.004$ on $K=1$), with almost no extra cost on never-seen tokens.
Layers 2 and 4 are equivalent (difference $+0.001 \pm 0.001$ with $K=1$, within noise); a fast part on all six MLPs hurts on every validation stream, and a smaller $\alpha$ ($3\times10^{-4}$) costs almost nothing shuffled but gains less in order.
On the long books the fast part raises the share of the 14M--31M gap closed from 50\% to 55\%.

\subsection{Learning the update rule}

We also learned one $\alpha$ and one $\lambda$ for each of 25 weight matrices (Meta-SGD \cite{li2017metasgd} at the level of tensors), starting from the best hand-picked configuration, with exact second-order meta-gradients through episodes of 16 chunks on 26 validation books.
The held-out loss improved by only 0.004 nats/token before stopping, and every learned $\alpha$ stayed within 0.8--1.34 times its starting value.
On the test streams the learned rule ties the hand-picked one with $K=1$ and on the long books and loses more shuffled ($-0.020$ against $-0.008$): the small fast parts on the other tensors add cost without adding gain.

\subsection{Long contexts and model size}

On longer books, online learning is worth a large part of a model-size step.
On 4 books of 200k tokens (Table~\ref{tab:verylong}, Figure~\ref{fig:verylong}), Pythia-14M learning online closes 49\% of the gap to a frozen Pythia-31M (51\% with the fast part), rising from 7\% in the first 10k tokens of a book to at most 79\% (84\% with the fast part) at 160k--170k, but it stays above frozen 31M at every position.
Pythia-31M learning online gains about as much as 14M does ($+0.168 \pm 0.013$ against $+0.170$ over each model's own frozen loss), stays $0.348 \pm 0.013$ ahead of 14M learning online, and beats a frozen Pythia-70M in 3 late bins, though not overall ($-0.054 \pm 0.010$).

Online learning does not favor the smaller model.
On the main stream the locality is the same at 14M and 31M overall ($+0.038$ and $+0.039$; difference $+0.000 \pm 0.001$), and slightly larger at 31M on recent same-book tokens ($+0.018 \pm 0.004$); at 70M it almost vanishes (Table~\ref{tab:size}).
At these sizes this does not support the hypothesis that smaller models rely more on a cache in their weights.
Pythia-70M is the exception: plain SGD barely helps it at any learning rate we tried (check-book gain 0.011 at its best rate $10^{-5}$, against 0.057 for 31M and 0.070 for 14M), so whether its weak locality is about size or about the optimizer remains open.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

The results support a qualified version of the cache picture.
Online learning gives a small language model a memory beyond its attention window; its long-range part is local, tied mostly to the current book rather than to the recency of single tokens, and stored in the MLPs below the output, consistent with feed-forward layers as key-value memories \cite{geva2021transformer} and with MLP weights as the adaptable part in recent test-time training \cite{feng2026inplace}.
General knowledge is only partly kept: tokens not seen recently are predicted slightly worse.
Turning the base weights into a more aggressive cache does not pay off at any locality we tested, because the cost on everything else grows much faster than the gain on recent material.
A small fast part on top of the slow learner has a better exchange rate, but its gain is small.
Context length matters more: over 200k-token books online learning is worth about half the step from 14M to 31M parameters, yet the larger model gains as much, so the cache does not substitute for size at these scales.

These are preliminary results with clear limitations.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One seed, few books.} Error bars are across books (25, or 5 and 4 for the long streams), not seeds; with 4--5 books the SE itself is uncertain.
\item \textbf{Plain SGD with per-run learning rates}, each picked from three or four values, several at the edge of their grid; comparisons across layers and sizes mix in the learning rate.
\item \textbf{Tuning data.} The first picks, including 31M's learning rate ($3\times10^{-4}$), were made on two test check books, not validation books.
\item \textbf{Overlapping books.} Two of the four 200k-token books, and all five 100k-token books, are also among the 25 books of the main stream (their first 20k tokens).
\item \textbf{Missing error bars.} The $K=5$ and $K=25$ runs saved no per-token losses and have no error bars.
\item \textbf{Three sizes from one family}, with a non-monotonic locality (flat from 14M to 31M, near zero at 70M).
\item \textbf{Capacity not tested}: the eviction test cannot separate forgetting a book from adapting to books in general.
\item \textbf{Wall time} is only a rough compute proxy: frozen runs batch 16 chunks, online runs process one chunk and one update at a time.
\end{itemize}

Next steps: interleaving many books chunk by chunk to measure how many contexts the weights can hold; an update normalized across layers or sizes, and more sizes, to understand Pythia-70M; and PolyPythias \cite{vanderwal2025polypythias}, which provides several pre-training seeds per size, for error bars across models.

\section{Conclusion}

A small language model that takes one SGD step on each chunk it reads gains a memory beyond its attention window: local, tied mostly to the current book, stored in the MLPs below the output, and more than a count cache beyond 1k tokens.
Making that memory more aggressive does not help; a small decaying fast part on one MLP, on top of the slow learner, helps a little and robustly.
On long books online learning is worth about half a model-size step, but larger models gain just as much from it.
